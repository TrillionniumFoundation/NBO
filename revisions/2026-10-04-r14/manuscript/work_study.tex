\section{Work, Costate Accuracy, and Implemented Decisions}\label{sec:r14work}
The fixed-work experiment complements the announced-time study with a comparison whose simulator workload does not depend on the worker's speed. It uses the same economic primitives and action radius, dimensions ten, twenty, and fifty, and two specified training streams. Each method runs eighty iterations. The exact twentieth and eightieth checkpoints are evaluated on a fresh bank of 8,192 paths and 1,024 cells. NBO, direct policy optimization, the affine actor, and raw-costate improvement use the same number of training states and rollout cells. The raw-costate method differentiates current-policy rollouts and improves an actor without fitting a critic. Its action class and rollout work agree with NBO's; its evaluation work does not include critic regression.

\subsection{Fixed work and attained economic precision}
One iteration uses 128 states and 32 rollout cells. Validation occurs every twenty iterations with 128 paths and 64 cells. The twentieth checkpoint therefore uses 81,920 training-state visits and 8,192 validation-state visits; the eightieth uses 327,680 and 32,768, respectively. NBO and raw-costate improvement perform five actor updates per iteration, while the two direct methods perform one. NBO also performs five critic updates. Equal simulator visits and equal iteration counts consequently do not imply equal floating-point work. The recorded update counts and clocks expose that distinction.

The common targets are schedule-improvement lower endpoints of zero, 0.0005, and 0.001. For each fitted stream, we inspect the declared checkpoints in temporal order and include verification of every earlier unsuccessful checkpoint. Each full verification advances both a policy and the common schedule for 16,777,216 state transitions. Reaching the second checkpoint therefore incurs two such verification calculations in addition to the training and validation visits. The full supplementary table retains unreached targets and reports the available clocks without assigning an artificial zero cost to failure.

There are two distinct timing interpretations. The executed experiment trained all eighty iterations before either checkpoint was evaluated; its recorded cost includes that complete fit. A retrospective prefix frontier uses the saved clock at the tested checkpoint and asks what an otherwise identical sequential procedure would have consumed. This frontier is informative about work allocation, but is not a measured early-stopping speedup. Moreover, the saved evaluation clock begins after weight loading and deterministic interval-account construction. Sums of those clocks measure recorded timed components. Exact simulator visits, update counts, full worker records, and the omitted clock boundaries are reported alongside them.

The second operating-system environment repeats dimension ten and the first fixed stream. It provides an execution-sensitivity check with the same source, nominal seeds, protocol, and work budgets. The initial-state hashes agree, but the saved innovation hashes differ across environments. Consequently, its endpoint differences are not treated as a common-path cross-environment estimator. Within each environment, every method comparison still uses exactly matching innovation and initial-state hashes. The supplement reports the weight, clock, and endpoint changes without treating the repeated environment as an additional optimizer-population observation.

\subsection{The observed evaluation--improvement mechanism}
The independent mechanism panels use sixty-four held-out states and sixteen nested rollout replicates, with thirty-two and 128 time cells. A critic predicts a costate for the frozen policy. Coarse rollout replicate zero supplies the raw-costate action. The common reference for the corrected comparisons is the mean of fine replicates one through fifteen, which is independent of that raw action's replicate and of the fitted critic. All three saved actions are evaluated against this same reference, and its constrained greedy action is recomputed from the primitive Hamiltonian. The original diagnostics and arrays are preserved together with the corrected reference calculation.

In all six principal dimension--stream panels, the critic's mean squared error exceeds the raw-costate error against the common independent reference. The ratio ranges from approximately 1.06 to 1.99. The critic-implied greedy action also has a larger reference Hamiltonian gap in every panel. These observations locate a restriction of the fitted evaluation block: its regression does not realize the variance-reduction opportunity in Proposition~\ref{prop:r12costate} for these states, budgets, and networks. They do not alter the independently verified policy payoffs. The direct NBO-minus-raw economic intervals in the accompanying table assess the resulting policies as a separate question.

The reference mean remains noisy and discretized. Its Monte Carlo variance and the change between nested rollout grids are reported separately. The held-out states follow the declared training-state design, rather than the improved policy's discounted occupation law. Thus these panels test the evaluation mechanism without substituting their empirical mean squared errors into the welfare inequality in Proposition~\ref{prop:r14economiccostate}. That proposition supplies the additional bias, occupation, and action-optimization terms needed for such a substitution.

\subsection{Independent reference and observation diagnostics}
The scalar calculation is replayed with the original frozen policies and all four original grids. An independent stencil calculation records each fixed-policy equation residual, the Howard residual and iteration count, boundary values, and action-bound frequencies at every time step. The replay reproduces all original arrays exactly in the recorded validation environment. The supplementary tables give value and action changes under refinement and policy losses at five distinct initial states.

On the finest domain-six grid, the NBO loss at initial state zero is approximately $3.44\times10^{-6}$, compared with $1.16\times10^{-4}$ for direct policy optimization. At initial state one, the corresponding losses are approximately $1.05\times10^{-4}$ and $5.02\times10^{-5}$. The off-center comparison therefore differs from the center comparison. Widening the domain from six to eight also changes the spatial step, and is reported as joint domain-and-spacing sensitivity. The high-dimensional optimum comparison continues to use its own explicit $G_d-L_a$ bound; scalar losses are not extrapolated to another dimension or controller.

The finite-observation experiment implements the direct measured-state policy of Proposition~\ref{prop:r14sensor}. It fixes the original eightieth NBO checkpoint in each dimension and uses 128 paths, sixty-four or 128 decision cells, and eight fine Euler cells per decision. Independent sensor noise has normalized root-mean-square levels zero, 0.001, and 0.01. Both compared controllers use the same protected saved-weight map. Their shared-noise payoff differences are finite-Euler diagnostics. The separate outward transfer calculation accounts for sensing, the internal recurrence, network arithmetic, and clipping in the original continuous diffusion. No diagnostic difference is substituted for a continuous-time endpoint, and no certificate from the 1,024-cell experiment is assigned to a different parent or grid.

These comparisons complete the evidence for the paper's economic distinctions. A policy may have a positive verified gain over a feasible schedule while its ranking against another trained policy remains statistically unresolved. A useful scalar approximation may have state-dependent relative accuracy. A critic may supply a coherent improvement direction while a cheaper costate estimator performs better on the declared diagnostic. The NBO error account states the quantities needed to assess each of these outcomes within the original economic problem.
