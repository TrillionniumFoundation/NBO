                                           1. INTRODUCTION
23                                                                                             23

24     Quantitative economic models increasingly place several margins of adjustment           24

25   inside the same dynamic decision problem. A household chooses consumption and             25

26   portfolio risk while investing in its own preferences. Recursive utility makes con- 26
27   tinuation utility part of the policy-evaluation equation. A sophisticated consumer        27

28   anticipates the decisions of future selves, while a firm invests in anticipation of its   28

29   rivals’ policies. These problems require a computational method that evaluates the        29

30   relevant continuation values and determines whether feasible changes in behav- 30

1    ior improve the stated economic objective. The motivating preference-formation           1

2    problem of Qi (2025) brings the internal and external margins together.                  2

3        This paper develops Neural Bellman Operators (NBO) around policy evalua- 3
4    tion, feasible improvement, and economic verification. A neural critic represents        4

5    continuation utility, and a feasible actor represents the controls entering the state    5

6    dynamics. The critic evaluates a fixed policy using its boundary conditions and          6

7    utility domain. The actor then improves its Hamiltonian with the critic and its          7

8    derivative channels held fixed. Independent verification measures the remaining          8

9    evaluation error and the gain from feasible deviations. These steps give the trained     9

10   networks an economic interpretation and specify the accuracy of the policy actually      10

11   implemented.                                                                             11

12       Our first contribution is a common evaluation and improvement formulation for        12

13   the economic applications. In smooth problems, a verification theorem translates         13

14   boundary error, fixed-policy residual error, and feasible-action error into value and    14

15   policy-loss bounds. For nonsmooth problems, a positive-weight Bellman formula- 15
16   tion supplies the monotonicity needed for viscosity selection. A guarded evaluation      16

17   map enforces a declared nodal tolerance and records the corrections required to          17

18   do so. Complete-action bounds use signed derivative enclosures and verified face         18

19   domination, including the effects of stopping switches. The representation can there- 19
20   fore be chosen for numerical flexibility while the economic acceptance criterion is      20

21   expressed in the model’s values and deviations.                                          21

22       The second contribution carries policy-specific verification through to a dense      22

23   nonlinear capital diffusion on its original unbounded state space. A feasible an- 23
24   alytical schedule supplies an absolute comparison with the economic optimum. 24
25   Curvature controls the benefit from changing consumption, and a spectral bound           25

26   controls the propagation of those changes through the coupled production process. 26
27   A paired payoff identity then measures the value of a fitted correction to the sched- 27
28   ule. Explicit transfer, tail, arithmetic, and sampling terms turn this comparison into   28

29   a policy-specific continuous-time bound. The comparison class includes the original      29

30   adapted controls. The candidate controller is specified by its update rule, mem- 30
31   ory, and information structure, so that the evaluated policy and the implemented         31

32   policy are the same mathematical object. A product-space argument shows that             32

1    independent private randomization preserves the economic value. An observation           1

2    construction implements the internal controller from exact state histories, and a        2

3    separate payoff-transfer theorem extends implementation to finite dates and noisy        3

4    state measurements.                                                                      4

5      The third contribution makes the role of the Bellman evaluation block testable. In     5

6    the capital economy, strong concavity bounds Hamiltonian loss by costate error and       6

7    actor suboptimality. A conditional-projection identity separates variation in rollout    7

8    costates from the error of a learned predictor. A performance-difference theorem         8

9    integrates the resulting decision losses along the candidate policy’s state distribu- 9
10   tion and separates costate regression error, rollout bias, and actor suboptimality       10

11   in a welfare-improvement inequality. These results identify the quantities through       11

12   which critic learning can improve an actor. The numerical study accordingly com- 12
13   pares NBO with direct policy optimization, affine policies, and critic ablations. 13
14   Direct method differences are formed on common simulated paths, with each fit- 14
15   ted candidate fixed before final testing. Comparisons report economic endpoints,         15

16   candidate-generation work, and verification work; checkpoint frontiers expose the        16

17   relation between accuracy and computation. The recorded initialization streams de- 17
18   scribe the fitted policies in the experiment, and the stated confidence event concerns   18

19   their independently evaluated payoffs.                                                   19

20     The original applications give these computational objects distinct economic           20

21   meanings. In the preference model, value gradients determine consumption and the         21

22   costly adjustment of risk aversion, while a cross derivative links portfolio demand      22

23   to preference risk. The policywise cost comparison establishes value monotonic- 23
24   ity in the adjustment cost, and a specified compensating transfer gives a welfare        24

25   interpretation to numerical error. Recursive utility is evaluated within a declared      25

26   invariant utility domain. For temporal selves, the actor satisfies a spike-deviation     26

27   condition against two endogenous continuation values. In the capital game, the           27

28   independent criterion is a complete unilateral best response against fixed rival         28

29   policies. The main text states this application structure, and the supplement gives      29

30   the full models, derivations, and numerical records. Each conclusion is attached to      30

31   the economic domain and approximation for which it is established.                       31

                                  1.1. Relation to the literature
14                                                                                              14

15       Neural residual methods build on numerical dynamic programming and function            15

16   approximation, not on a new principle of optimality. Projection and related methods        16

17   exploit economic structure to approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). 17
18   Derivative approximation by smooth neural networks provides useful represen- 18
19   tation results (Hornik et al., 1990), but does not establish that a particular finite      19

20   architecture or optimizer solves a nonlinear boundary-value problem.                       20

21       The differential critic is closely related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano     21

22   and Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary          22

23   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also        23

24   includes an HJB application. The distinguishing object here is the combination of          24

25   an explicit feasible actor, block-specific evaluation, and economic error accounting       25

26   across control and equilibrium problems. An analytical formula for the maximizing          26

27   action is helpful when available, but is not a universal dividing line between neural      27

28   PDE methods. Likewise, recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth- 28
29   order term; full nonlinearity in the PDE sense concerns the highest derivatives.           29

30       Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein     30

31   and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Its infinite-horizon formulation requires        31

32   particular care about utility-process selection and transversality; Herdegen et al. 32
                                    NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                5

1    (2021) explains why a stationary algebraic solution alone is insufficient. The time- 1
2    inconsistency extension is an equilibrium problem in the sense of Björk et al. (2016),      2

3    not an ordinary control problem with a relabelled flow payoff. Finally, stochastic          3

4    approximation results require their own step-size, stability, and limiting-dynamics         4

5    conditions (Borkar, 2024). They do not convert constant-step Adam into a conver- 5
6    gence theorem for economic optimality.                                                      6

7      The inherited continuous-capital result closes a different part of the error account:     7

8    curvature and a verified coupling norm bound the gain from arbitrary adapted                8

9    deviations, while a schedule-centred actor supplies a uniformly constrained neural          9

10   policy. The resulting bound concerns the original diffusion rather than a nodal             10

11   surrogate. Its stronger structural benchmark is reported alongside every fitted             11

12   policy.                                                                                     12

                                   3.3. Conditioning and stopping
22                                                                                            22

23     A positive, parameter-independent state weighting can precondition the critic          23

24   equation without changing its zero set. If that weight depends on learned quanti- 24
25   ties, its derivative must be included in the stated critic objective. The homothetic     25

26   laboratory divides the Hamiltonian by its positive coefficient and differentiates that   26

27   entire normalized critic residual. In the actor step the same coefficient is detached. 27
28   Error conversion to the original economic equation includes the scale factor.            28

29     A zero residual target does not make learning dynamics stationary. For the scalar      29

30   loss ℓ(z) = a2 z2 /2, gradient descent is stable only when 0 < ηa2 < 2. Likewise, full   30

31   differentiation of δ2 /2 gives δ∇δ, while a semi-gradient TD update differentiates       31

32   only the current-value term. The two computations must not be compared using             32
                                NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                11

1    inconsistent signs or discount conventions. If f already includes −ρv, a time dis- 1
2    cretization must not add a second independent discount to the same continuation          2

3    equation.                                                                                3

4      Constant-step Adam is a numerical choice in the calibration laboratory, not an         4

5    application of a two-timescale theorem. A genuine two-timescale analysis would           5

6    require nonsummable and square-summable step sizes, an actor-to-critic ratio             6

7    tending to zero, controlled noise, bounded iterates, and suitable attracting limiting    7

8    dynamics (Borkar, 2024). A stationary critic gradient need not imply a zero residual,    8

9    and a stationary multi-agent gradient need not imply Nash optimality. The results        9

10   below therefore concern verified approximation errors rather than an assumed             10

11   universal optimizer convergence property.                                                11

                                       13. CONCLUSION
13                                                                                            13

14     Neural Bellman Operators provide an evaluation and improvement structure for           14

15   continuous-time economic control and equilibrium problems. The critic approxi- 15
16   mates the continuation value associated with a specified policy; the actor makes         16

17   feasible choices using that value; and independent verification measures the remain- 17
18   ing economic error. Differential verification, monotone Bellman approximation, and       18

19   complete-action enclosures make these roles explicit across the models considered        19

20   here.                                                                                    20

21     The capital application connects a fitted policy to the original diffusion through     21

22   a policy-specific payoff comparison. The analytical schedule supplies an abso- 22
23   lute benchmark, and direct paired inference measures the gain from a particular          23

24   learned policy after accounting for implementation and sampling error. Costate           24

25   accuracy provides a mathematical link between critic evaluation and Hamiltonian          25

26   improvement. The computational comparisons assess that link against direct policy        26

27   optimization and other feasible alternatives, while reporting the work required both     27

28   to construct and to verify each candidate.                                               28

29     The economic program remains the one that motivates NBO. Endogenous prefer- 29
30   ences combine consumption, portfolio risk, and costly internal adjustment. Recur- 30
31   sive utility changes the continuation-value equation. Temporal selves and compet- 31
32   ing firms require equilibrium-specific deviation conditions. Their applications retain   32
     62

1    their own models, derivations, economic comparisons, and verification results. The                           1

2    resulting framework makes quantitative claims assessable in utility, compensa- 2
3    tion, and unilateral-profit units, with the information structure and approximation                          3

4    domain stated for each claim.                                                                                4

5      The theoretical and computational results identify where further precision is valu- 5
6    able: policy evaluation must improve the derivatives relevant for decisions, and the                         6

7    resulting gain must justify the total work and implementation cost. This connection                          7

8    between a numerical Bellman calculation and a verified economic decision is the                              8

9    organizing contribution of the paper.                                                                        9
