1                                       1. INTRODUCTION                                         1

2                                                                                               2

3        An economic decision maker often changes a current choice while leaving its con- 3
4    tinuation problem unchanged. A tax or consumption constraint may change today,             4

5    a portfolio manager may face a new current mandate, or an optimizer may revisit            5

6    a nearby state. The continuation value is a common economic object across these            6

7    decisions. Learning it can replace repeated continuation calculations by evaluations       7

8    of one function. The relevant question is not whether the function interpolates its        8

9    training observations, but whether its marginal values improve feasible decisions          9

10   enough to justify learning and deploying it.                                               10

11       This paper develops Neural Bellman Operators (NBO) around that question. The           11

12   operator separates policy evaluation from policy improvement. A continuation               12

13   representation is fitted while the reference policy is held fixed. The actor then uses     13

14   detached continuation channels to choose a feasible policy. The distinction matters        14

15   both analytically and computationally: a joint derivative through the actor and            15

16   its moving evaluation target is not the same map. A final independent economic             16

17   comparison assesses the returned policy. The framework applies to continuous- 17
18   time control and, under their additional assumptions, recursive utility, endogenous        18

19   preference formation, temporal selves, and dynamic games. These applications               19

20   and their original derivations remain in the current supplement; the quantitative          20

21   method comparisons concern the capital economy developed in the article.                   21

22       The first contribution is an error account from a learned continuation to a feasible   22

23   policy and its continuous-economy payoff. The analysis distinguishes a fixed-policy        23

24   numerical continuation from the limiting Bellman solution, and a held-action con- 24
25   troller from a continuously updated feedback. The feasible analytical schedule             25

26   supplies an explicit economic anchor. Candidate-occupation error, maximization             26

27   error, holding loss, arithmetic, and diffusion transfer enter different terms. The gen- 27
28   eral evaluation, viscosity, and improvement results are stated with the assumptions        28

29   that support them, rather than inferred from a small training residual.                    29

30       The second contribution is a signed occupation account. A norm-based sufficient        30

31   condition replaces the continuation correction by a worst-case magnitude and can           31

32   lose the cancellation that actually occurs along the policy’s action changes. We           32
                                 NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                 3

1    instead evaluate common-noise continuation differences and form predicted im- 1
2    provement minus the signed correction before statistical inference. The expectation       2

3    is exactly the finite Bellman gain. An antithetic range calculation then transfers a      3

4    fresh lower endpoint to the continuous economy. In the executed assessment, the           4

5    original negative norm-based bounds coexist with positive signed lower bounds             5

6    of at least 0.000767 and 0.000776 in dimensions ten and fifty. This is evidence           6

7    that the returned NBO policies realize positive occupation Bellman gains, not an          7

8    identification of the critic’s unique incremental contribution relative to Raw.           8

9      The third contribution concerns the economic value of the common continuation           9

10   itself. We state precisely when a rollout, its innovations, and its derivative can be     10

11   reused. For a scalar-gradient dictionary fixed independently of the fitting labels, the   11

12   reduction in prediction risk is exactly removed noise variance less approximation         12

13   cost. A quadratic-decision specialization translates the same decomposition into a        13

14   payoff comparison. These results exhibit a positive economic mechanism for learn- 14
15   ing, including strict improvement when the dictionary is correctly specified and          15

16   removes relevant noise. They also show why the mechanism cannot be assumed:               16

17   a misspecified representation may cost more than the variance it removes, and a           17

18   Raw competitor using the same projection receives the same benefit. The completed         18

19   continuation-menu comparison gives every method its legitimate reuse rather than          19

20   forcing Raw to regenerate observations unnecessarily. Across four finite-period           20

21   designs, two dimensions, and three fixed work stages, it retains 128 trials and 640       21

22   method fits. At the last stage, the long fifty-dimensional case has material NBO          22

23   payoff advantages over the fitted vector costate, Raw actor, and direct actor. In six     23

24   of the eight final-stage economic–dimension cases, the entire NBO-minus-cached- 24
25   sample-average interval lies inside the prespecified practical-equivalence band,          25

26   while NBO uses less construction and query work. In both long-continuation cases,         26

27   the cached sample-average optimizer has a material payoff advantage. Thus the ex- 27
28   periment identifies an economic work–accuracy tradeoff, not universal dominance           28

29   by a learned representation.                                                              29

30     The numerical evidence makes three distinctions essential to the economic in- 30
31   terpretation. First, independent confirmation of a fixed policy is different from         31

32   retraining a method on new streams. A new prospective bank confirms the original          32

                                  1.1. Relation to the literature
22                                                                                            22

23       Neural residual methods build on numerical dynamic programming and function          23

24   approximation, not on a new principle of optimality. Projection and related methods      24

25   exploit economic structure to approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). 25
26   Derivative approximation by smooth neural networks provides useful represen- 26
27   tation results (Hornik et al., 1990), but does not establish that a particular finite    27

28   architecture or optimizer solves a nonlinear boundary-value problem.                     28

29       The differential critic is closely related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano   29

30   and Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary        30

31   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also      31

32   includes an HJB application. The distinguishing object here is the combination of        32
                                    NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                5

1    an explicit feasible actor, block-specific evaluation, and economic error accounting        1

2    across control and equilibrium problems. An analytical formula for the maximizing           2

3    action is helpful when available, but is not a universal dividing line between neural       3

4    PDE methods. Likewise, recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth- 4
5    order term; full nonlinearity in the PDE sense concerns the highest derivatives.            5

6      Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein        6

7    and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Its infinite-horizon formulation requires         7

8    particular care about utility-process selection and transversality; Herdegen et al. 8
9    (2021) explains why a stationary algebraic solution alone is insufficient. The time- 9
10   inconsistency extension is an equilibrium problem in the sense of Björk et al. (2016),      10

11   not an ordinary control problem with a relabelled flow payoff. Finally, stochastic          11

12   approximation results require their own step-size, stability, and limiting-dynamics         12

13   conditions (Borkar, 2024). They do not convert constant-step Adam into a conver- 13
14   gence theorem for economic optimality.                                                      14

15     The article proceeds from the economic problem to its evaluation and improve- 15
16   ment map. Section A states the general verification results. Sections 4–A.2 specialize      16

17   the welfare account to the capital economy. Section B states the other economic             17

18   applications. The computational study distinguishes the existing comparison from            18

19   the current experiment. The supplement collects the complete models, derivations,           19

20   proofs, and supporting diagnostics by subject.                                              20

15                                                                                              15

17   L a V } = 0. We use an infinite-horizon result only after specifying admissibility, 17

5                                                                                                         5

6        For a box [ a, ā], a sigmoid map produces interior feasible actions but does not                6

7    attain its endpoints at finite weights. Binding constraints can instead be represented               7

8    by a projection onto the closed interval, by an explicit boundary candidate, or by a                 8

9    finite action set including the endpoints. The constrained portfolio calculation uses a              9

10   projection and can attain a zero risky share exactly. On a simplex, a probability map                10

11   supplies a feasible interior point and boundary faces must again be considered when                  11

12   certifying the optimum. General constraints require a feasible parameterization, a                   12

13   certified projection, or a constrained action solver. A finite penalty is not an exact               13

14   feasibility certificate.                                                                             14

16                                                                                           16

17     The general evaluation and improvement results are collected in the supplement,       17

18   with their complete statements and proofs retained. Fixed-policy evaluation, the        18

19   selection of a Bellman solution, and the performance of the returned actor are          19

20   separate claims. Their hypotheses specify admissibility, comparison, regularity or      20

21   monotone approximation, and the relevant terminal or transversality condition. 21
22   Recursive utility, endogenous preferences, temporal selves, and dynamic games           22

23   retain their own hypotheses; the capital experiment does not provide numerical          23

24   validation for those distinct applications.                                             24

25     The capital development below specializes this chain to explicit feasible actions,    25

26   a tractable schedule, and a payoff statistic in economic units. It then separates two   26

27   questions. The first is whether the selected policy produces a positive gain. The       27

28   second is whether learning the continuation improves decisions or work relative         28

29   to a credible procedure without that scalar critic. The signed occupation account       29

30   addresses the first question. The common-continuation and decision-risk results         30

31   describe a mechanism for the second, which still requires an appropriate empirical      31

32   comparison for the implemented nonlinear methods.                                       32
