                            GUIDE TO THE ECONOMIC APPLICATIONS
7                                                                                              7

8      This document is the current economic-applications companion to Neural Bellman          8

9    Operators. It retains the complete application text of the reviewed article and sup- 9
10   plement, including model primitives, boundary and utility restrictions, analytical        10

11   derivations, equilibrium conditions, implemented numerical procedures, and their          11

12   recorded successes and failures. It is organized by economic problem. Its section,        12

13   equation, table, and theorem numbers are distinct from those in the main article          13

14   and technical supplement; cross-references connect all three documents.                   14

15     The applications share a policy-evaluation and feasible-improvement construc- 15
16   tion. For a given policy, the critic solves that policy’s continuation equation under     16

17   the stated terminal, exit, or reflection conditions. The actor changes only the actions   17

18   whose deviations define the relevant economic problem. A control problem com- 18
19   pares feasible policies, a sophisticated consumer compares local deviations against       19

20   future selves, and a firm compares complete unilateral deviations against fixed           20

21   rivals. A small fitted residual is not substituted for any of these economic criteria.    21

22     The homothetic portfolio and recursive tests use a known invariant representa- 22
23   tion and report its scope explicitly. The endogenous-preference application retains       23

24   the role of cardinal normalization across controlled preference states, the reflected     24

25   preference domain, the cross derivative in portfolio choice, and the envelope com- 25
26   parison for adjustment costs. Its continuous-action records retain actor failures,        26

27   local-search contributions, whole-domain off-grid failures, fresh-grid training, and      27

28   the separate consumption-compensation experiment. The temporal-self condition             28

29   concerns infinitesimal deviations; the game calculations retain missing pure fit- 29
30   ted stage equilibria and approximate best-response bounds. The coupled linear- 30
31   quadratic laboratory has an exactly representable structure. These distinctions are       31

32   substantive economic restrictions rather than interchangeable numerical labels.           32

     A.1.2.1. A well-defined validation problem at the retained parameters                               13

14     The numerical calibration uses (A.5) as a finite-horizon matching-bequest test, not               14

15   as an unqualified infinite-horizon theorem. Choose T > 0, stop wealth on leaving                    15

16   [ x, x̄ ] with 0 < x < x̄ < ∞, and impose the candidate Kx b /b on the stopping bound-              16

17   ary and at T. Consumption shares are in a compact positive interval containing                      17

18   0.0455, and portfolio shares are in a compact interval containing 0.3.                              18

19     For these retained parameters, a > 0 and b < 0. Since c ranges over a compact                     19

20   positive interval, there exist 0 < Y < Y < ∞ such that f (c, Y/b) ≤ 0 and f (c, Y/b) ≥              20

21   0 for every feasible c. Indeed, c a Y −a/b tends to zero as Y ↓ 0 and to infinity as Y ↑ ∞.         21

22   Enlarge the interval so that it contains the boundary candidate. The constants Y/b                  22

23   and Y/b are respectively lower and upper barriers for the backward utility equation. 23
24   Scalar BSDE comparison therefore keeps every policy utility in this interval. The                   24

25   aggregator is Lipschitz there. The smooth homothetic candidate maximizes the                        25

26   Hamiltonian and matches all stopping payoffs; Theorem S.1 identifies it as the value                26

27   of this specified finite-horizon problem.                                                           27

28     The choice of a matching bequest is an analytical validation device. It does                      28

29   not establish that the same candidate is selected by every infinite-horizon recur- 29
30   sive formulation. A separate infinite-horizon application must check its utility- 30
31   process class and transversality, particularly when risk aversion and intertemporal- 31
32   complementarity parameters straddle one (Herdegen et al., 2021). The paper retains                  32

12                                                                                                 12
       Two experiments distinguish action representation from action optimization.
13                                                                                                 13
     First, the finite-policy study trains two-hidden-layer, width-32 tanh critics and
14                                                                                                 14
     categorical actors. At each backward step it computes the complete 175-action
15                                                                                                 15
     payoff vector using the current continuation critic. Its exact argmax provides a label,
16                                                                                                 16
     and the vector also supplies the actor’s expected action-loss objective. Thus the
17                                                                                                 17
     training oracle is exhaustive, even when the final actor is accurate. It is a useful neural
18                                                                                                 18
     policy-representation experiment, but not evidence that the actor avoided Bellman
19                                                                                                 19
     maximization.
20                                                                                                 20
       Second, the continuous-action study trains a continuous-output actor directly
21                                                                                                 21
     through its own positive-weight payoff. It never receives an all-action value vector
22                                                                                                 22
     or an exact argmax label. The actor receives 160 Adam updates per time level; the
23                                                                                                 23
     continuation critic receives 320 Adam updates and up to 300 L-BFGS iterations, with
24                                                                                                 24
     failed polishing steps rolled back. The actor-free direct comparator optimizes eight
25                                                                                                 25
     independent bounded controls at every state for the same 160 iterations, followed
26                                                                                                 26
     by the same critic fit. Every restart and rejected action query is charged. Neither
27                                                                                                 27
     procedure invokes a Riccati solution, an analytically invariant policy class, or the
28                                                                                                 28
     independently computed reference during training.

                      A.1.15. Strategic Deviations and Missing Pure Stages                      8

9                                                                                               9

10     For a fixed Markov rival profile in a finite-horizon finite-state/action game, condi- 10
11   tion on any history. The own player’s conditional payoff from a randomized current         11

12   action is a convex combination of pure-action payoffs with the same continuation           12

13   best-response values. It cannot exceed the largest such payoff. Backward induction         13

14   therefore bounds every randomized and history-dependent unilateral policy by the           14

15   Markov pure-action best-response recursion. Outward propagation then provides              15

16   a bound over this full deviation class, not merely the particular local deviations         16

17   observed during actor training.                                                            17

18     Existence of a pure equilibrium in a fitted one-step game is a different matter. 18
19   If none exists, minimum-defect selection produces a feasible profile, not an exact         19

20   equilibrium. The independent dynamic best response still evaluates that profile            20

21   correctly. The following records give every missing pure stage in the six principal        21

22   finite-policy study neural runs, including time index, state, and minimum maximum          22

23   stage defect. Indices begin at zero. The six runs have 6 × 30 × 225 = 40,500 active        23

24   state-time nodes; the five recorded exceptions are not excluded from their final           24

25   dynamic bounds.                                                                            25

27   policy, shortlist policy, and corrected finite lookup policy are different deployment      27

28   objects. The principal finite-policy study guard fraction is zero, but the full joint- 28
29   action oracle was nevertheless constructed during training. A mixed-strategy profile       29
