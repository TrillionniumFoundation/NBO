                                           1. INTRODUCTION                                        23

24     An economic decision often requires a continuation value that must itself be               24

25   computed. A household changes consumption and portfolio risk while investing                 25

26   in its preferences. A sophisticated consumer anticipates future selves, and a firm           26

27   invests against the policies of its rivals. These problems differ in their utility domains   27

28   and deviation criteria, but each requires an evaluation of the consequences that             28

29   follow a proposed action. The preference-formation problem of Qi (2025) provides             29

30   a motivating example in which internal and external choices interact.                        30

1        This paper develops Neural Bellman Operators (NBO), an evaluation and im- 1
2    provement map for these economic problems. A critic estimates the continuation             2

3    value of a specified policy. A feasible actor uses its derivative channels to change       3

4    current decisions, keeping the evaluated continuation fixed. The resulting policy is       4

5    assessed against an explicit economic criterion. Boundary conditions, information,         5

6    numerical approximation, and the deployed decision rule are part of that criterion. 6
7        The central question is when learning a continuation value makes economic              7

8    computation more effective. A fitted continuation can be reused across states and          8

9    action queries, but fitting is costly and its approximation error can alter the selected   9

10   action. A credible comparison must allow a simulation-based method to cache the            10

11   information that the same decision problem permits. It must also include the cost          11

12   of training and choosing the continuation representation. We therefore study the           12

13   accuracy and complete work of the learned evaluation block when several economic           13

14   decisions use a common continuation. The nonlinear capital economy provides the            14

15   common primitives for this comparison and for the policy-specific error analysis.          15

16       The theory follows the evaluation–decision–payoff chain. A fixed-policy equation       16

17   identifies the continuation being approximated. The improvement map specifies              17

18   the actor’s feasible action and the derivatives it can use. General verification results   18

19   distinguish an evaluation defect from unfinished action maximization. For the              19

20   capital economy, an exact finite Bellman identity places the relevant continuation         20

21   error on the action change under the candidate’s own occupation law. A signed              21

22   continuation calculation can assess the same identity using common future paths,           22

23   retaining the cancellation between predicted improvement and prediction error. 23
24   Its payoff range depends on the economy and action tube. The continuous-time               24

25   account separately charges the implementation, diffusion, clipping, and numerical          25

26   errors of the held controller. These are error bounds for computed objects; they do        26

27   not assert a convergence rate for a neural optimizer.                                      27

29   repeated-decision study examines the incremental value of reusing a learned contin- 29

                                  1.1. Relation to the literature
21                                                                                         21

22       Neural Bellman Operators build on numerical dynamic programming and func- 22
23   tion approximation. Projection and related methods exploit economic structure to      23

24   approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). Smooth neural networks can           24

25   approximate derivatives under representation hypotheses (Hornik et al., 1990). 25
26   These results distinguish representational capacity from the accuracy achieved by a   26

27   finite architecture and a particular optimizer.                                       27

28       The differential critic is related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano and    28

29   Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary         29

30   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also   30

31   includes an HJB application. Our object combines a specified policy continuation,     31

32   a feasible actor, and an economic error account, and asks when the continuation       32
                                    NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                5

1    representation is useful across decision queries. An analytical maximizing action is        1

2    useful when available; it is not a universal distinction between neural PDE methods. 2
3    Recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth-order term, whereas full            3

4    nonlinearity of a PDE concerns its highest derivatives.                                     4

5      Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein        5

6    and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Utility-process selection and transver- 6
7    sality matter in its infinite-horizon formulation (Herdegen et al., 2021). Temporal         7

8    selves instead require an equilibrium formulation (Björk et al., 2016). The stochastic      8

9    approximation literature provides convergence results under explicit step-size, sta- 9
10   bility, and limiting-dynamics conditions (Borkar, 2024). We retain those distinctions       10

11   when describing a finite neural computation.                                                11

12     Section 2 defines the economic object and Section 4 specializes it to the capital         12

13   economy. Section 4.3 connects continuation error with economic gain. The compu- 13
14   tational design in Section 5 distinguishes continuation reuse, conditional payoff           14

15   replication, and mechanism assessment. Section 7 states the other economic appli- 15
16   cations. General verification results and their full proofs remain in the technical         16

17   supplement, beginning with Section S.1; the application companion is a current              17

18   part of the paper.                                                                          18

13     The algorithm produces a continuation approximation and a feasible policy. Its       13

14   economic interpretation requires an error account for each. The general results in     14

15   technical Section S.1 bound the consequence of a fixed-policy evaluation error and     15

                                       8. CONCLUSION
12                                                                                            12

13     Neural Bellman Operators connect a specified continuation object with feasible         13

14   economic decisions. The nonlinear capital economy makes this connection explicit:        14

15   an exact finite Bellman identity accounts for continuation error along the chosen        15

16   action change, while a separate diffusion account assesses the deployed continuous- 16
17   time policy. Repeated decisions permit the same continuation representation to be        17

18   used across economic tasks. A fixed gradient-dictionary calculation identifies the       18

19   approximation–variance tradeoff of a linear projection, and the numerical experi- 19
20   ment measures the economic performance of the nonlinear fitted procedure.                20

21     The complete continuation-menu experiment establishes material NBO payoff              21

22   gains over materialized Raw and direct-policy actors in two previously unpiloted         22

23   fifty-dimensional economies. NBO also uses less construction and query work              23

24   than direct policy in those cells. At the final prescribed SAA budget, six of eight      24

25   economic cells have equivalent payoffs within 10−4 and lower construction and            25

26   query work for NBO. Cached SAA has materially higher payoffs in both longest- 26
27   horizon cells. The full set of earlier budgets, cheaper vector and Raw actors, and       27

28   common confirmation costs remains part of the evidence. This result identifies           28

29   specific economic gains from a reusable fitted continuation and the work needed to       29

30   obtain them.                                                                             30

31     Independent confirmation supports the original-policy paired comparison under          31

32   a prospectively fixed analysis. The strengthened HJB procedure shows why the             32
                                       NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                            43

1    implementation must remain explicit: NBO’s material advantage over both new                                1

2    HJB deployments is established in the low-volatility, fifty-dimensional economy,                           2

3    while the remaining direct comparisons are unresolved. A new signed continuation                           3

4    statistic verifies positive occupation Bellman gains by retaining the common-path                          4

5    cancellation. These positive payoff statements coexist with the reported full-class                        5

6    regret bounds and the finite menu’s separate scalar accuracy account.                                      6

7      The economic applications retain their own utility domains, boundary conditions,                         7

8    and deviation criteria. These determine the continuation that must be learned and                          8

9    the decision that constitutes an improvement. The current application compan- 9
10   ion preserves the complete models, derivations, proofs, and numerical records for                          10

11   recursive utility, endogenous preferences, temporal selves, and strategic interac- 11
12   tion. The common evaluation–improvement structure makes their computational                                12

13   requirements comparable while retaining their distinct economic content.                                   13
