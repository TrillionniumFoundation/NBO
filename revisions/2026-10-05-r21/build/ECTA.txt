25                                          1. INTRODUCTION                                        25

26                                                                                                 26
       A current economic choice changes a future that is costly to evaluate. A household
27                                                                                                 27
     chooses consumption while investing in its preferences; a firm adjusts capital
28                                                                                                 28
     against the policies of its rivals; an economist recomputes these choices under
29                                                                                                 29
     alternative charges or constraints. Neural Bellman Operators (NBO) evaluate a
30
     specified continuation policy and use a feasible actor to improve current decisions. 30
31                                                                                                 31

32     Qian QI: qiqian@pku.edu.cn                                                                  32
     2

1    The question is when paying for a learned continuation makes these decisions             1

2    more accurate, or less expensive at a specified economic accuracy, than directly         2

3    simulating or approximating them.                                                        3

4        This question involves three distinct objects. The continuation equation specifies   4

5    the future being valued. The actor specifies the feasible decision actually taken. The   5

6    economic error account specifies what is known about its payoff. A small regression      6

7    loss does not, by itself, answer the economic question; neither does a short training    7

8    clock. The comparison must charge construction, all action queries, unsuccessful         8

9    accuracy checks, and the verification required to return a decision. It must also        9

10   allow competing procedures to reuse the information that their decision problems         10

11   permit.                                                                                  11

12       The paper develops this evaluation–decision–payoff chain for controlled              12

13   economies. Its original differential and monotone formulations, recursive utility, en- 13
14   dogenous preferences, temporal selves, and strategic interaction remain substantive      14

15   components. Policy evaluation is always policy specific. A utility-domain restric- 15
16   tion cannot be discarded when the approximation is reused, and a best response           16

17   against fixed rivals is not a proof of equilibrium. These distinctions determine which   17

18   continuation can be shared and when it must be refreshed.                                18

19       Our first contribution is an explicit connection between continuation reuse and      19

20   decision loss. A centered error, rather than an arbitrary level error, controls the      20

21   loss from an approximate maximizing action. A transport account then bounds              21

22   the additional loss when future economic primitives change. The resulting reuse          22

23   region includes bounded changes in transitions, rewards, and recursive evaluation        23

24   operators; it is not confined to tasks with an identical future. The bound makes         24

25   the relevant stability and utility-domain conditions explicit. It also explains why      25

26   prediction-risk rankings need not coincide with the payoff rankings of competing         26

27   policies.                                                                                27

28       Our second contribution is a work-to-accuracy implementation of this account. 28
29   For a finite-support instance of the nonlinear capital economy, an outward interval      29

30   calculation certifies the true objective’s derivative and a uniform strong-concavity     30

31   bound. Every returned action is assessed against the full continuous scalar action       31

32   interval, not merely a computed candidate list. A frozen sequence of fitting budgets     32

1    stops at the first successful economic check and charges every attempt. This supplies    1

2    a realized stopping cost, whereas the earlier simultaneous catalogue closure supplies    2

3    a valid but retrospective accuracy comparison. Both calculations remain in the paper. 3
4      The completed design varies the number of decisions from one to 1,024 in di- 4
5    mensions ten and fifty. Temporary withdrawal charges, current utility weights,           5

6    adjustment costs, and caps generate the decisions while the future reference pol- 6
7    icy stays fixed. Seven complete procedures include NBO, conventional quadratic           7

8    and radial-basis continuation regressions, a shared task-conditioned actor, cached       8

9    sample-average optimization, full-support enumeration, and a current-reward              9

10   rule. Every dimension–volume cell is executed under three fixed fitting and cache        10

11   streams. The integrated execution replays this already frozen design; it does not        11

12   create additional independent streams by repeating a computation. The resulting          12

13   economic certificates are deterministic for the stored finite economy.                   13

14     NBO reaches the prescribed scalar-interval tolerance in all 36 of its complete runs. 14
15   This identifies successful learned-continuation decisions, not uniform neural superi- 15
16   ority. The non-neural surrogates and simulation-based procedures also achieve the        16

17   target and remain on the measured cost frontier. The shared actor and current-only       17

23   predictor.                                                                               23

                                 1.1. Relation to the literature
14                                                                                          14

15     Neural Bellman Operators build on numerical dynamic programming and func- 15
16   tion approximation. Projection and related methods exploit economic structure to       16

17   approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). Smooth neural networks can            17

18   approximate derivatives under representation hypotheses (Hornik et al., 1990). 18
19   These results distinguish representational capacity from the accuracy achieved by a    19

20   finite architecture and a particular optimizer.                                        20

21     The differential critic is related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano and       21

22   Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary          22

23   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also    23

24   includes an HJB application. Our object combines a specified policy continuation,      24

25   a feasible actor, and an economic error account, and asks when the continuation        25

26   representation is useful across decision queries. An analytical maximizing action is   26

27   useful when available; it is not a universal distinction between neural PDE methods. 27
28   Recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth-order term, whereas full       28

29   nonlinearity of a PDE concerns its highest derivatives.                                29

30     Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein   30

31   and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Utility-process selection and transver- 31
32   sality matter in its infinite-horizon formulation (Herdegen et al., 2021). Temporal    32
     6

1    selves instead require an equilibrium formulation (Björk et al., 2016). The stochastic     1

2    approximation literature provides convergence results under explicit step-size, sta- 2
3    bility, and limiting-dynamics conditions (Borkar, 2024). We retain those distinctions      3

4    when describing a finite neural computation.                                               4

5        Section 5 defines the economic object and Section S.31 specializes it to the capi- 5
6    tal economy. Section S.31.3 connects continuation error with economic gain. The            6

7    computational design in Section S.2 distinguishes continuation reuse, conditional          7

8    payoff replication, and mechanism assessment. Section 10 states the other economic         8

9    applications. General verification results and their full proofs remain in the technical   9

10   supplement, beginning with Section S.5; the application companion is a current             10

11   part of the paper.                                                                         11

                   1.2. Continuation approximation and the contribution of NBO
14                                                                                              14

15       A reusable continuation is not a new principle of dynamic programming. 15
16   Postdecision-state approximation separates a controlled transition from the subse- 16
17   quent uncertainty and is a standard device in approximate dynamic programming              17

18   (Powell, 2011). Regression can estimate a continuation relevant to an economic             18

19   stopping decision, as in Longstaff and Schwartz (2001). Batch fitted value methods         19

20   turn transition data into successive supervised regression problems (Ernst et al.,         20

21   2005); approximate policy iteration likewise separates an evaluation error from an         21

22   imperfect improvement step (Scherrer et al., 2015). These methods establish the            22

23   appropriate comparison class for NBO. The relevant question is not whether con- 23
24   tinuation fitting is possible, but which fitted object, decision rule, and verification    24

25   account are useful for the stated economic exercise.                                       25

26       The actor–critic distinction is also established. In Konda and Tsitsiklis (1999),      26

27   a critic supplies information for a parameterized policy update under explicit             27

28   stochastic-approximation conditions. NBO uses a scalar policy continuation and its         28

29   derivative channels in a feasible economic improvement map. The scalar represen- 29
30   tation couples value and gradient predictions through one potential; a separately          30

31   fitted vector field need not have this property. That structural distinction is testable   31

32   but does not imply that a scalar neural network must outperform a costate surro- 32
                                     NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                  7

1    gate, a direct actor, or a conventional scalar regression. The menu’s vector baseline          1

2    and the new quadratic, radial-basis, and shared-actor baselines therefore remain in            2

3    the primary comparisons.                                                                       3

4      The reuse comparison is closely related to simulation optimization. A cached                 4

5    sample-average objective can be optimized at many current tasks. Reusing inno- 5
6    vations is legitimate; reusing their payoff at a different postdecision state without          6

7    reevaluation is not. The new experiment gives cached simulation and regression                 7

8    the same finite-law information. The regressions share their value and gradient                8

9    labels, and the shared actor receives the same task variables. Full-support enumera- 9
10   tion is a particularly strong comparator when the law has only sixteen atoms. Its              10

11   tractability is disclosed rather than suppressed to manufacture an advantage for               11

12   approximation.                                                                                 12

13     Several mathematical components are standard. Centering removes constants                    13

14   from action comparisons; the squared-error difference cancels common noise;                    14

15   shortest-path closure solves difference constraints; and a strong-concavity bound              15

16   converts a first-order residual into an objective gap. For stochastic stopping, time- 16
17   uniform confidence sequences provide a general route to valid repeated assessment              17

18   (Howard et al., 2021). We do not claim any of these principles as a new theorem of             18

19   dynamic programming, graph theory, or probability. The contribution developed                  19

20   here is their explicit economic linkage: which continuation is reusable, how approxi- 20
21   mation and changes in future primitives enter decision loss, which computed action             21

22   is certified, and which work is actually paid before that certificate is returned. The         22

23   deterministic finite-law experiment uses no confidence sequence and spends no                  23

24   additional probability. Its scalar-interval guarantee and the earlier statistical menu         24

25   guarantee are different applications of that linkage.                                          25

                                       11. CONCLUSION
11                                                                                            11

12     Neural Bellman Operators organize policy evaluation, feasible improvement,             12

13   and economic assessment around a specified continuation. The contribution is an          13

14   economic computation with an explicit error and work account, not a new principle        14

15   of optimality or a presumption that a neural approximation must dominate a               15

16   conventional one.                                                                        16

17     The centered decision-loss bound identifies the errors that can change current         17

18   choices. The continuation-transport theorem extends reuse to a verified neighbor- 18
19   hood of future economic primitives and gives a condition for refreshing the evalu- 19
20   ated object. The scalar true-objective certificate then supports actual first-success    20

21   stopping with every failed check charged. These results connect approximation,           21

22   economic stability, verification, and amortization without conflating their distinct     22

23   assumptions.                                                                             23

24     The capital evidence contains both successful NBO decisions and useful rever- 24
25   sals. The original high-dimensional menus establish protected payoff advantages          25

26   in declared cells, while other cells favor simulation and simpler actors. The new        26

27   query-volume study certifies NBO across every declared stream and retains the            27

28   competitive conventional surrogates, unresolved actor outcomes, full verification        28

29   bill, and own-action diagnostics. A repetition of the same frozen study is a repli- 29
30   cation of its computation, not a larger independent experiment. The finite scalar        30

31   certificate does not substitute for the separate continuous-time and full-control        31

32   accounts.                                                                                32
