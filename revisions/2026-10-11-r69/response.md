## M5 and B4: Precision-based original policy-cost inference

The new Bellman-centered theorem addresses the resolving-power problem directly. For a frozen policy, the discounted sum of original optimal Bellman advantages along its actual trajectory has expectation equal to its policy loss. For two policies with the same initial law, the initial optimal value cancels in expectation, leaving exactly the original expected policy-cost difference.

The verified local regret contract bounds this observation by the sum of local error budgets. Thus the statistical range can be much smaller than the range of realized cumulative economic costs. This is not subtraction of two regret bounds: sampled, paid optimal-advantage enclosures determine the mean, and the theorem identifies its expectation with the cost difference. Nor is the identity asserted path by path for realized cash costs. Both statements have exact finite-model regression tests.

The new inference protocol fixes 1,024 independent initial path rows per task, forty-eight-bit continuous bins, an evaluator tolerance of `target/32`, and a normalized loss margin of `1/256` before observing the contrasts. Numerical and sampling half-width allocations are each `1/512`. The original R67 ReLU model is loaded without retraining, alongside the new certificate-trained readouts and adaptive search. Every pairwise comparison is retained. Ordinary cash-cost intervals use the same paths, making the precision tradeoff visible.

Finer Bellman evaluation is charged. The raw width ledger reports conditional Bellman enclosure, state-box transfer, unresolved acquisition and final arithmetic components. The state-box term combines bin width with propagated state arithmetic; it is not falsely decomposed into separately identified estimates. Valid support intersections can reduce the final width, so pre-intersection components need not add to the contracted interval. Empty intersections stop verification, and acquisition-ambiguous paths are retained conservatively. Numerical and statistical allocation failures are reported rather than triggering a sign-dependent increase in sample size.

The `1/256` margin is an explicit tolerance in the original normalized economic objective. It is not presented as an estimated monetary willingness to pay. Equivalence or noninferiority is claimed only when the corresponding simultaneous interval satisfies this predetermined margin; neither claim establishes exact equality or strict neural superiority.

## M9 and B9: The economic purchase and its precision

The original policy-cost and information-purchase problem is retained. The new theorem makes the required precision an explicit computational purchase: tighter original-optimum evaluations and a finite independent sample produce a policy-difference interval at a declared scale. This can support noninferiority at a specified loss tolerance even when a strict expected-cost ordering is not identified.

For a cost-difference interval `[l,u]`, measured work difference `Delta W`, computation price `p` and replacement fee `f`, net replacement cost lies in `[l+p*Delta W+f,u+p*Delta W+f]`. A negative upper endpoint licenses replacement under those declared prices. When work saving is positive, a noninferiority upper bound gives a sufficient price threshold rather than a claim of a measured monetary gain. When the observed work ordering is unfavorable, the manuscript does not manufacture a purchase case.

No empirical monetary calibration is added. The application remains the original theoretical investment economy, with normalized losses and decision-maker prices clearly identified. The substantive new object is a constructive and paid precision account for the original policy comparison, alongside a matched training and complete-work experiment. It is not a claim that the economic interpretation alone reverses an adverse neural timing result.
