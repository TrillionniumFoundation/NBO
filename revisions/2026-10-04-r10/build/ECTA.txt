                                           1. INTRODUCTION
24                                                                                             24

25     Quantitative economic models increasingly place several margins of adjustment           25

26   inside the same dynamic decision problem. An investor chooses consumption and             26

27   risk exposure while managing a preference state. A sophisticated consumer chooses         27

28   today knowing that tomorrow’s self will reconsider. A firm invests while antici- 28
29   pating the investment policies of its rivals. These problems share a computational        29

30   structure: a continuation value must correctly evaluate a policy, and that policy must    30

31                                                                                             31

32     Qian QI: qiqian@pku.edu.cn                                                              32
     2

1    satisfy the appropriate optimality or equilibrium condition. The two requirements           1

2    are distinct. A perfectly evaluated policy need not be optimal, and an attractive           2

3    policy evaluated by an inaccurate continuation value need not improve welfare.              3

4        This paper develops Neural Bellman Operators (NBO) around this distinction. A           4

5    critic represents continuation utility. A feasible actor represents the controls that       5

6    enter the economic generator. The critic is fitted to a policy-evaluation equation,         6

7    including its terminal and boundary data. Holding the critic and its derivative chan- 7
8    nels fixed, the actor improves its own Hamiltonian. An independent maximization             8

9    step measures the remaining feasible-action gap. The resulting error decomposition          9

10   concerns economic values and deviations, rather than only parameter gradients or            10

11   the scalar quantity minimized by an optimizer.                                              11

12       The motivation remains continuous-time economic control, including recursive            12

13   utility and costly preference formation in the motivating model of Qi (2025). We do         13

14   not replace these problems by a prediction task. Instead, we make explicit how a            14

15   neural approximation is connected to the original decision problem. In the smooth           15

16   case, the connection follows from a verification bound. In the nonsmooth case, it           16

17   follows from a monotone Bellman approximation and a comparison principle. The               17

18   actor and critic may be trained by different numerical procedures in either case. 18
19   Their economic interpretation is determined by the equations they satisfy and by            19

20   the errors that are verified after training.                                                20

21       The paper makes three connected contributions. First, it specifies a neural policy- 21
22   evaluation and improvement map with explicit derivative routing, boundary condi- 22
23   tions, and an indexed evaluation guard. The guard bounds a selected-policy target           23

24   residual by construction; it does not claim convergence of Adam or an uncorrected           24

25   network. Second, it makes the complete-action error account operational through             25

26   signed interval derivatives and verified face domination, with natural union bounds         26

27   at stopping switches. A matched enclosure experiment isolates this calculation from         27

28   policy training. Third, it carries the accounts through the original preference, capital,   28

29   and strategic applications: nested-grid retraining, a model-specific compensating           29

30   transfer, an absolute continuous-time capital benchmark, and finite dynamic best            30

31   responses. Comparison, monotonicity, interval inclusion, and approximate policy             31

32   iteration remain inherited principles. The new results concern the specified guarded        32
                                 NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                3

1    map, its verified output, and its measured economic accuracy, not an unqualified         1

2    claim that a neural actor dominates classical or actor-free alternatives.                2

3      The numerical program uses generic multilayer critics and actors as well as clearly    3

4    separated analytical laboratories. The finite preference and game studies train actors   4

5    using exhaustively constructed continuation-payoff labels; their results establish       5

6    neural policy representation, not avoided maximization. The continuous-action            6

7    preference study instead differentiates the actor’s own payoff, records local im- 7

                                 1.1. Relation to the literature
20                                                                                            20

21     Neural residual methods build on numerical dynamic programming and function            21

22   approximation, not on a new principle of optimality. Projection and related methods      22

23   exploit economic structure to approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). 23
24   Derivative approximation by smooth neural networks provides useful represen- 24
25   tation results (Hornik et al., 1990), but does not establish that a particular finite    25

26   architecture or optimizer solves a nonlinear boundary-value problem.                     26

27     The differential critic is closely related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano     27

28   and Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary        28

29   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also      29

30   includes an HJB application. The distinguishing object here is the combination of        30

31   an explicit feasible actor, block-specific evaluation, and economic error accounting     31

32   across control and equilibrium problems. An analytical formula for the maximizing        32
     4

1    action is helpful when available, but is not a universal dividing line between neural       1

2    PDE methods. Likewise, recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth- 2
3    order term; full nonlinearity in the PDE sense concerns the highest derivatives.            3

4        Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein      4

5    and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Its infinite-horizon formulation requires         5

6    particular care about utility-process selection and transversality; Herdegen et al. 6
7    (2021) explains why a stationary algebraic solution alone is insufficient. The time- 7
8    inconsistency extension is an equilibrium problem in the sense of Björk et al. (2016),      8

9    not an ordinary control problem with a relabelled flow payoff. Finally, stochastic          9

10   approximation results require their own step-size, stability, and limiting-dynamics         10

11   conditions (Borkar, 2024). They do not convert constant-step Adam into a conver- 11
12   gence theorem for economic optimality.                                                      12

13       The continuous-capital result closes a different part of the error account: curvature   13

14   and a verified coupling norm bound the gain from arbitrary adapted deviations,              14

15   while a schedule-centred actor supplies a uniformly constrained neural policy. The          15

16   resulting bound concerns the original diffusion rather than a nodal surrogate. Its          16

17   stronger structural benchmark is reported alongside every fitted policy.                    17

18       Section 2 specifies the economy. Section 3 defines the implemented map and              18

19   Section 4 establishes its error accounts. Section 5 completes the differential theorem– 19
20   algorithm–experiment chain. The subsequent sections retain and extend the port- 20
21   folio, recursive-utility, preference-adjustment, temporal-self, dynamic-game, and           21

22   scaling applications. The appendix proves the principal results. The supplement             22

23   gives arithmetic details, application-specific assumptions, every seed-level compar- 23
24   ison, and sensitivity results.                                                              24

28                                                                                            28

29     A positive, parameter-independent state weighting can precondition the critic          29

30   equation without changing its zero set. If that weight depends on learned quanti- 30
31   ties, its derivative must be included in the stated critic objective. The homothetic     31

32   laboratory divides the Hamiltonian by its positive coefficient and differentiates that   32
     10

1    entire normalized critic residual. In the actor step the same coefficient is detached. 1
2    Error conversion to the original economic equation includes the scale factor.            2

3      A zero residual target does not make learning dynamics stationary. For the scalar      3

4    loss ℓ(z) = a2 z2 /2, gradient descent is stable only when 0 < ηa2 < 2. Likewise, full   4

5    differentiation of δ2 /2 gives δ∇δ, while a semi-gradient TD update differentiates       5

6    only the current-value term. The two computations must not be compared using             6

7    inconsistent signs or discount conventions. If f already includes −ρv, a time dis- 7
8    cretization must not add a second independent discount to the same continuation          8

9    equation.                                                                                9

10     Constant-step Adam is a numerical choice in the calibration laboratory, not an         10

11   application of a two-timescale theorem. A genuine two-timescale analysis would           11

12   require nonsummable and square-summable step sizes, an actor-to-critic ratio             12

13   tending to zero, controlled noise, bounded iterates, and suitable attracting limiting    13

14   dynamics (Borkar, 2024). A stationary critic gradient need not imply a zero residual,    14

15   and a stationary multi-agent gradient need not imply Nash optimality. The results        15

16   below therefore concern verified approximation errors rather than an assumed             16

17   universal optimizer convergence property.                                                17
