3        This paper studies Neural Bellman Operators (NBO), a numerical evaluation and          3

4    improvement map for these problems. A critic evaluates the continuation value              4

5    of a specified policy. A feasible actor changes the actions entering the economic          5

6    generator, holding that value and its derivative channels fixed. An independent            6

7    error account evaluates the resulting policy. The numerical object includes its bound- 7
8    ary conditions, utility domain, approximation scheme, information structure, and           8

9    deployment rule. These restrictions are part of the method: they determine what            9

10   the computed policy and its reported accuracy mean.                                        10

11       The economic role of the critic is the central question. Learning a continuation       11

12   value can pool information across states and actions, but the fitting cost and approx- 12
13   imation bias may exceed the benefit of that reuse. A positive policy gain does not         13

14   establish that its critic was useful. Nor does an accurate derivative at training states   14

15   establish an improvement along the distribution generated by the deployed candi- 15
16   date. We connect evaluation error to feasible action quality and then to economic          16

17   payoffs, keeping the distribution, the evaluation target, and the numerical transfer       17

18   explicit. The computational comparisons examine the same chain through direct              18

19   alternatives to the learned evaluation block.                                              19

20       The analysis has three parts. First, the NBO specification separates policy eval- 20
21   uation from action improvement and states the boundary and utility conditions              21

22   needed by both. Differential and positive-weight Bellman formulations give dis- 22
23   tinct numerical realizations of this map. Their error accounts distinguish policy- 23
24   evaluation defects from incomplete action maximization; the monotone formulation           24

25   supplies a viscosity-selection argument under its stated approximation hypothe- 25
26   ses. These results concern the output errors of a numerical method, rather than            26

27   convergence of a particular neural optimizer.                                              27

28       Second, a nonlinear capital economy permits a detailed connection between              28

29   the computed controller and the original continuous-time problem. Log felicity,            29

30   bounded consumption shares, additive diffusion, and bounded coupled produc- 30
31   tion yield an analytical comparison policy and explicit propagation bounds. A              31

32   policy-specific payoff identity, diffusion transfer, and simultaneous common-path          32

                                 1.1. Relation to the literature
8                                                                                             8

9      Neural residual methods build on numerical dynamic programming and function            9

10   approximation, not on a new principle of optimality. Projection and related methods      10

11   exploit economic structure to approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). 11
12   Derivative approximation by smooth neural networks provides useful represen- 12
13   tation results (Hornik et al., 1990), but does not establish that a particular finite    13

14   architecture or optimizer solves a nonlinear boundary-value problem.                     14

15     The differential critic is closely related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano     15

16   and Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary        16

17   conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also      17

18   includes an HJB application. The distinguishing object here is the combination of        18

19   an explicit feasible actor, block-specific evaluation, and economic error accounting     19

20   across control and equilibrium problems. An analytical formula for the maximizing        20

21   action is helpful when available, but is not a universal dividing line between neural    21

22   PDE methods. Likewise, recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth- 22
23   order term; full nonlinearity in the PDE sense concerns the highest derivatives.         23

24     Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein     24

25   and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Its infinite-horizon formulation requires      25

26   particular care about utility-process selection and transversality; Herdegen et al. 26
27   (2021) explains why a stationary algebraic solution alone is insufficient. The time- 27
28   inconsistency extension is an equilibrium problem in the sense of Björk et al. (2016),   28

29   not an ordinary control problem with a relabelled flow payoff. Finally, stochastic       29

30   approximation results require their own step-size, stability, and limiting-dynamics      30

31   conditions (Borkar, 2024). They do not convert constant-step Adam into a conver- 31
32   gence theorem for economic optimality.                                                   32

24       For a box [ a, ā], a sigmoid map produces interior feasible actions but does not        24

25   attain its endpoints at finite weights. Binding constraints can instead be represented       25

15     Neural Bellman Operators organize economic computation around the continua- 15
16   tion object that is evaluated, the feasible action that it changes, and the economic                    16

17   consequence of that change. The separation matters for recursive utility, prefer- 17
18   ence adjustment, temporal selves, and strategic interaction because each problem                        18

19   has its own evaluation equation and deviation criterion. The approximation and                          19

20   verification arguments make those distinctions explicit.                                                20

21     The capital economy permits an exact finite Bellman account under the candi- 21
22   date’s occupation law, a computable feasible-actor gap, and a quantified transfer to                    22

23   the original continuous diffusion. These results make the evaluation–action–payoff                      23

24   chain executable without identifying a finite-rollout derivative with a continuous- 24
25   time costate. The independent paired transfer also makes use of the actual common                       25

26   innovations in direct method comparisons, while preserving the original observa- 26
27   tions and confidence event.                                                                             27

28     The complete sixteen-stream experiment establishes economically material NBO                          28

29   payoff gains over the declared neural HJB implementation in both ten and fifty                          29

30   dimensions. In dimension fifty, NBO reaches the prescribed online gain target in                        30

31   every stream with less measured complete work than direct policy optimization. 31
32   Cached Raw remains cheaper; the direct NBO–Raw and NBO–DPO payoff intervals                             32

5    and numerical allowances, but its sufficient lower bound does not certify positive                         5
