                                           1. INTRODUCTION
24                                                                                              24

25     A current economic choice changes a future that is costly to evaluate. A household       25

26   chooses consumption while investing in its preferences; a firm adjusts capital             26

27   against the policies of its rivals; an economist recomputes these choices under            27

28   alternative charges or constraints. Neural Bellman Operators (NBO) evaluate a              28

29   specified continuation policy and use a feasible actor to improve current decisions. 29
30   The question is when paying for a learned continuation makes these decisions               30

31                                                                                              31

32     Qian QI: qiqian@pku.edu.cn                                                               32
     2

1    more accurate, or less expensive at a specified economic accuracy, than directly         1

2    simulating or approximating them.                                                        2

3        This question involves three distinct objects. The continuation equation specifies   3

4    the future being valued. The actor specifies the feasible decision actually taken. The   4

5    economic error account specifies what is known about its payoff. A small regression      5

6    loss does not, by itself, answer the economic question; neither does a short training    6

7    clock. The comparison must charge construction, all action queries, unsuccessful         7

8    accuracy checks, and the verification required to return a decision. It must also        8

9    allow competing procedures to reuse the information that their decision problems         9

10   permit.                                                                                  10

11       The paper develops this evaluation–decision–payoff chain for controlled              11

12   economies. Its original differential and monotone formulations, recursive utility, en- 12
13   dogenous preferences, temporal selves, and strategic interaction remain substantive      13

14   components. Policy evaluation is always policy specific. A utility-domain restric- 14
15   tion cannot be discarded when the approximation is reused, and a best response           15

16   against fixed rivals is not a proof of equilibrium. These distinctions determine which   16

17   continuation can be shared and when it must be refreshed.                                17

18       Our first contribution is an explicit connection between continuation reuse and      18

19   decision loss. A centered error, rather than an arbitrary level error, controls the      19

20   loss from an approximate maximizing action. A transport account then bounds              20

21   the additional loss when future economic primitives change. The resulting reuse          21

22   region includes bounded changes in transitions, rewards, and recursive evaluation        22

23   operators; it is not confined to tasks with an identical future. The bound makes         23

24   the relevant stability and utility-domain conditions explicit. It also explains why      24

25   prediction-risk rankings need not coincide with the payoff rankings of competing         25

26   policies.                                                                                26

27       Our second contribution is a work-to-accuracy implementation of this account. 27
28   For a finite-support instance of the nonlinear capital economy, an outward interval      28

29   calculation certifies the true objective’s derivative and a uniform strong-concavity     29

30   bound. Every returned action is assessed against the full continuous scalar action       30

31   interval, not merely a computed candidate list. A frozen sequence of fitting budgets     31

32   stops at the first successful economic check and charges every attempt. This supplies    32
                                 NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                 3

1    a realized stopping cost, whereas the earlier simultaneous catalogue closure supplies     1

2    a valid but retrospective accuracy comparison. Both calculations remain in the paper. 2
3      The completed design varies the number of decisions from one to 1,024 in di- 3
4    mensions ten and fifty. Temporary withdrawal charges, current utility weights,            4

5    adjustment costs, and caps generate the decisions while the future reference pol- 5
6    icy stays fixed. Seven complete procedures include NBO, conventional quadratic            6

7    and radial-basis continuation regressions, a shared task-conditioned actor, cached        7

8    sample-average optimization, full-support enumeration, and a current-reward               8

9    rule. Every dimension–volume cell is executed under three fixed fitting and cache         9

10   streams. The integrated execution replays this already frozen design; it does not         10

11   create additional independent streams by repeating a computation. The resulting           11

12   economic certificates are deterministic for the stored finite economy.                    12

13     NBO reaches the prescribed scalar-interval tolerance in all 36 of its complete runs. 13
14   This identifies successful learned-continuation decisions, not uniform neural superi- 14
15   ority. The non-neural surrogates and simulation-based procedures also achieve the         15

16   target and remain on the measured cost frontier. The shared actor and current-only        16

17   procedure retain their unsuccessful checks. Common exogenous actions and each             17

18   predictor’s own selected actions are both assessed against full-support continuation      18

19   means; caches are regenerated across the three streams. Thus absolute risk, centered      19

20   risk, and actual finite-menu decision loss can be compared without treating simula- 20
21   tion noise as the target value or selecting the diagnostic action pair in favor of one    21

22   predictor.                                                                                22

24   them. In the previously unpiloted fifty-dimensional Long and Intermediate menus,          24

25   the protected NBO payoff advantages over materialized Raw and direct-policy               25

                                  1.1. Relation to the literature
28                                                                                              28

29       Neural Bellman Operators build on numerical dynamic programming and func- 29
30   tion approximation. Projection and related methods exploit economic structure to           30

31   approximate a Bellman equation; see Judd (1998). Smooth neural networks can                31

32   approximate derivatives under representation hypotheses (Hornik et al., 1990). 32
                                 NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                  5

1    These results distinguish representational capacity from the accuracy achieved by a        1

2    finite architecture and a particular optimizer.                                            2

3      The differential critic is related to the neural PDE approach in Sirignano and           3

4    Spiliopoulos (2018). Their DGM formulation includes differential and boundary              4

5    conditions and treats HJB equations. The deep BSDE method of Han et al. (2018) also        5

6    includes an HJB application. Our object combines a specified policy continuation,          6

7    a feasible actor, and an economic error account, and asks when the continuation            7

8    representation is useful across decision queries. An analytical maximizing action is       8

9    useful when available; it is not a universal distinction between neural PDE methods. 9
10   Recursive dependence on the value is a nonlinear zeroth-order term, whereas full           10

11   nonlinearity of a PDE concerns its highest derivatives.                                    11

12     Recursive utility separates intertemporal substitution from risk aversion (Epstein       12

13   and Zin, 1989, Duffie and Epstein, 1992). Utility-process selection and transver- 13
14   sality matter in its infinite-horizon formulation (Herdegen et al., 2021). Temporal        14

15   selves instead require an equilibrium formulation (Björk et al., 2016). The stochastic     15

16   approximation literature provides convergence results under explicit step-size, sta- 16
17   bility, and limiting-dynamics conditions (Borkar, 2024). We retain those distinctions      17

18   when describing a finite neural computation.                                               18

19     Section 4 defines the economic object and Section S.27 specializes it to the capi- 19
20   tal economy. Section S.27.3 connects continuation error with economic gain. The            20

21   computational design in Section 6 distinguishes continuation reuse, conditional            21

22   payoff replication, and mechanism assessment. Section 11 states the other economic         22

23   applications. General verification results and their full proofs remain in the technical   23

24   supplement, beginning with Section S.1; the application companion is a current             24

25   part of the paper.                                                                         25

                 1.2. Continuation approximation and the contribution of NBO
28                                                                                              28

29     A reusable continuation is not a new principle of dynamic programming. 29
30   Postdecision-state approximation separates a controlled transition from the subse- 30
31   quent uncertainty and is a standard device in approximate dynamic programming              31

32   (Powell, 2011). Regression can estimate a continuation relevant to an economic             32
     6

1    stopping decision, as in Longstaff and Schwartz (2001). Batch fitted value methods         1

2    turn transition data into successive supervised regression problems (Ernst et al.,         2

3    2005); approximate policy iteration likewise separates an evaluation error from an         3

4    imperfect improvement step (Scherrer et al., 2015). These methods establish the            4

5    appropriate comparison class for NBO. The relevant question is not whether con- 5
6    tinuation fitting is possible, but which fitted object, decision rule, and verification    6

7    account are useful for the stated economic exercise.                                       7

8        The actor–critic distinction is also established. In Konda and Tsitsiklis (1999),      8

9    a critic supplies information for a parameterized policy update under explicit             9

10   stochastic-approximation conditions. NBO uses a scalar policy continuation and its         10

11   derivative channels in a feasible economic improvement map. The scalar represen- 11
12   tation couples value and gradient predictions through one potential; a separately          12

13   fitted vector field need not have this property. That structural distinction is testable   13

14   but does not imply that a scalar neural network must outperform a costate surro- 14
15   gate, a direct actor, or a conventional scalar regression. The menu’s vector baseline      15

16   and the new quadratic, radial-basis, and shared-actor baselines therefore remain in        16

17   the primary comparisons.                                                                   17

18       The reuse comparison is closely related to simulation optimization. A cached           18

19   sample-average objective can be optimized at many current tasks. Reusing inno- 19
20   vations is legitimate; reusing their payoff at a different postdecision state without      20

21   reevaluation is not. The new experiment gives cached simulation and regression             21

22   the same finite-law information. The regressions share their value and gradient            22

23   labels, and the shared actor receives the same task variables. Full-support enumera- 23
24   tion is a particularly strong comparator when the law has only sixteen atoms. Its          24

25   tractability is disclosed rather than suppressed to manufacture an advantage for           25

26   approximation.                                                                             26

27       Several mathematical components are standard. Centering removes constants              27

28   from action comparisons; the squared-error difference cancels common noise;                28

29   shortest-path closure solves difference constraints; and a strong-concavity bound          29

30   converts a first-order residual into an objective gap. For stochastic stopping, time- 30
31   uniform confidence sequences provide a general route to valid repeated assessment          31

32   (Howard et al., 2021). We do not claim any of these principles as a new theorem of         32
                                     NEURAL BELLMAN OPERATORS                                       7

1    dynamic programming, graph theory, or probability. The contribution developed                       1

2    here is their explicit economic linkage: which continuation is reusable, how approxi- 2
3    mation and changes in future primitives enter decision loss, which computed action                  3

4    is certified, and which work is actually paid before that certificate is returned. The              4

5    deterministic finite-law experiment uses no confidence sequence and spends no                       5

6    additional probability. Its scalar-interval guarantee and the earlier statistical menu              6

7    guarantee are different applications of that linkage.                                               7

                                      12. CONCLUSION
4                                                                                            4

5      Neural Bellman Operators organize policy evaluation, feasible improvement,            5

6    and economic assessment around a specified continuation. The contribution is an         6

7    economic computation with an explicit error and work account, not a new principle       7

8    of optimality or a presumption that a neural approximation must dominate a              8

9    conventional one.                                                                       9

10     The centered decision-loss bound identifies the errors that can change current        10

11   choices. The continuation-transport theorem extends reuse to a verified neighbor- 11
12   hood of future economic primitives and gives a condition for refreshing the evalu- 12
13   ated object. The scalar true-objective certificate then supports actual first-success   13

14   stopping with every failed check charged. These results connect approximation,          14

15   economic stability, verification, and amortization without conflating their distinct    15

16   assumptions.                                                                            16

17     The capital evidence contains both successful NBO decisions and useful rever- 17
18   sals. The original high-dimensional menus establish protected payoff advantages         18

19   in declared cells, while other cells favor simulation and simpler actors. The new       19

20   query-volume study certifies NBO across every declared stream and retains the           20

21   competitive conventional surrogates, unresolved actor outcomes, full verification       21

22   bill, and own-action diagnostics. A repetition of the same frozen study is a repli- 22
23   cation of its computation, not a larger independent experiment. The finite scalar       23

24   certificate does not substitute for the separate continuous-time and full-control       24

25   accounts.                                                                               25

26     Recursive utility, endogenous preferences, temporal selves, and dynamic games         26

27   remain part of the paper. Their utility domains, information sets, transversality con- 27
28   ditions, and deviation criteria determine the continuations that can be evaluated and   28

29   reused. The complete application models and proofs are retained. Taken together,        29

30   the results specify when a learned scalar continuation supports an economically         30

31   accurate decision, when transport or refresh is required, and how to compare its        31

32   complete computational cost with other legitimate numerical procedures.                 32
