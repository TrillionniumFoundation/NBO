\section{Exact service-economy contracts}
Regimes are indexed by $i=0,\ldots,n-1$. Demand, rewards, installed actions, costs, and initial masses are defined in the main article. Ties in reward maximization use the lowest action index. All inputs are rational. The three regime laws are as follows. In the banded law, $P_{i,i+1}=P_{i+1,i}=(1+(i\bmod3))/16$ and $P_{ii}$ is the remaining row mass; other entries are zero. The cyclic law has $P_{ii}=3/4$ and $P_{i,(i+1)\bmod n}=1/4$. The dense law has $P_{ij}=1/(2n)+(1/2)\ind\{i=j\}$. Each is stochastic and independent of the action. These definitions, including the installed rule and initial distribution, are reconstructed independently by the checker.

The continuous state space consists of $n$ disjoint copies of $[0,1)^2$ equipped with their Borel sigma fields; the half-open convention assigns each modulo boundary a unique image. If $S_1(u,v)=(u+v^2\bmod1,v)$, then for every bounded Borel $h$, Fubini's theorem and translation invariance on the circle give $\int h(S_1(u,v))\,du\,dv=\int h(u,v)\,du\,dv$. The analogous identity holds for $S_2(u,v)=(u,v+u^2\bmod1)$. Their composition preserves uniform measure. Conditional on each possible regime successor, this is precisely the pushforward identity required by the main aggregation theorem. Its proof concerns arbitrary bounded Borel test functions, not only partition indicators. Pointwise regime transition probabilities give the other aggregation identity. The transition from an individual state is nonetheless a finite sum of Dirac masses.

The within-cell density alternative is $1+\zeta f(u)f(v)$, where $f(u)=2u_1-1$ on $[0,1)^2$. Since $\int f=0$ and $\int|f|=1/2$, it is a probability density for $|\zeta|\leq1$, and its source average is the uniform destination law. For a fixed source, its $L_1$ difference from the reset density is $|\zeta f(u)|/2$. The supremum over sources is $|\zeta|/2$ (a supremum need not be attained on the half-open cell). This is the operator norm over test functions bounded by one. Probability total variation is half this quantity. In both models the specified initial mixture uses exactly the same uniform reference measures; no approximate initial-law substitution is made.

\section{An exact tail bridge for discounted infinite-horizon revision}
The main computational experiments are finite horizon. There is also a simple infinite-horizon extension with an explicit terminal trust boundary. Consider time-homogeneous bounded primitives, finite actions, and $0<\beta<1$. Let $V^\infty$ be the exact operating fixed point and let $a^*$ be a Borel stationary maximizing selector. Allow common Markov policies to be time dependent, and impose $J_t^p(x)\geq V^\infty(x)-\varepsilon$ for every date and state. Denote this infinite-horizon minimum by $K_R^\infty$. Let $v_T$ be the finite-horizon revision minimum with terminal operating payoff $V^\infty$ and zero terminal implementation cost.
\begin{proposition}[Tail enclosure]
For any initial law and $\varepsilon\geq0$,
\[
 v_T\leq K_R^\infty\leq v_T+\frac{K\beta^T}{1-\beta}.
\]
Thus a finite certificate $[L_T,U_T]$ gives the infinite-horizon certificate $[L_T,U_T+K\beta^T/(1-\beta)]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take an arbitrary infinite-horizon feasible policy. Replacing its continuation from date $T$ by the operating-optimal selector weakly improves operating values at earlier dates. Its unchanged prefix is therefore feasible for the finite problem with terminal payoff $V^\infty$, and its truncated implementation cost does not exceed its full nonnegative cost. Taking infima proves the lower bound. Conversely, append $a^*$ to any finite feasible prefix. This supplies an operating-feasible common Markov policy at all dates and adds at most $K\beta^T/(1-\beta)$ implementation cost. Approximate minimizers prove the upper bound without assuming a Borel function-space optimizer exists.
\end{proof}
This proposition does not give a stationary-policy optimum: the admissible prefix is time dependent. It also does not treat an approximate terminal value as exact, or an average-cost problem as discounted. A numerical use requires a certified terminal fixed point and tail policy, or additional witness errors. No infinite-horizon execution is claimed in the tables.

\section{Data-dependent repair accounting}
For any two finite-horizon common policies $p,q$, define the implementation action values $Q^p_{k,t}(x,a)=k_t(x,a)+\beta P_t^aC^p_{t+1}(x)$. Their exact performance difference under the state law induced by $q$ is
\[
 \int(C_0^q-C_0^p)d\nu
 =\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\beta^t\E_q\left[\sum_a(q_t(a\mid X_t)-p_t(a\mid X_t))Q^p_{k,t}(X_t,a)\right].
\]
Subtract the two cost Bellman equations and iterate the resulting $q$-transition recursion to prove the identity. If $q_t=(1-\alpha_t)p_t+\alpha_t\delta_{a_t}$, its additional cost is at most the discounted $q$-occupancy average of $\alpha_t$ times the action span of $Q^p_{k,t}$. For a given finite repaired policy this can be much sharper than $D_C\sup\alpha_t$. The generic stopping theorem still needs a uniform bound over unexplored policies; a favorable realized repair cost does not replace that requirement. The new structural cases use exact primal evaluation, and do not charge an unnecessary worst-case repair cost to their actual upper endpoints.
