\section{Conclusion}
The economic content of a numerical policy depends on more than its training objective. Evaluation, feasible improvement, stochastic transition, boundary treatment, and equilibrium deviations are distinct objects. Neural Bellman Operators make their interfaces explicit and permit approximation errors to be related to policy value.

The finite-horizon error account shows how to combine critic, actor, boundary, and operator errors without identifying a small sampled residual with optimality. Feasible candidate comparison and convex improvement provide two ways to turn a neural proposal into a checked economic decision. The constrained stochastic resource experiment implements the latter and obtains independent numerical bounds on policy cost loss. The preference economy implements the former while retaining all internal and external controls, correlated shocks, and a complete liquidation contract. Its comparative-static theorem identifies a monotone aggregate effort object even when individual policy coordinates do not move monotonically.

Recursive utility, temporal selves, and dynamic games require their own continuation and admissibility conventions. Their analytical and computational tests support these extensions without conflating an algebraic target, a trained representation, and an equilibrium certificate. This combination of an economic model, an implemented operator, and an explicit error account is the basis on which additional dimensions, richer preferences, or structural estimation can be developed and evaluated.
