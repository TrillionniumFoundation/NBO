\section{Budgeted Learning, State Coverage, and Economic Decisions}\label{sec:r12study}
The new study separates the performance of a learned candidate from the incremental value of the Bellman evaluation block. The primitive capital economy, its action box, discounting, terminal payoff and dense coupling construction are unchanged. The fixed protocol uses dimensions 10, 20 and 50 and ten new initialization/minibatch streams. It is fixed after exploratory development and before the final fits and final simulation bank; it is not represented as a historical preregistration.

\subsection{Design and interpretation}
The primary population is a uniform distribution over nine balance-sheet profiles $y=\mu\bm1+sv_d$, with $\mu\in\{-0.5,0,0.5\}$, $s\in\{0,0.25,0.5\}$ and a fixed centered unit-root-mean-square linear profile $v_d$. The mean changes the aggregate level of initial capital, while $s$ changes cross-sectional dispersion. This transparent design is distinct from both the single favorable origin and the deliberately severe state stresses. It is not fitted to data. Training and validation draw from this population and from subsequent-state perturbations. The final simulation independently redraws the initial profile and its future shocks. We also retain a separate origin comparison to make the change from the earlier study visible.

Each method receives the same announced 20-second fitting budget on the same worker for a given seed. The budget covers initialization, optimization, current-policy rollouts, validation and checkpoint writes. The worker completes its current iteration; realized time and overshoot are reported rather than hidden. NBO performs policy evaluation with rollout value and costate targets, then feasible Hamiltonian improvement. Direct policy optimization differentiates the same actor's rollout payoff without a learned critic. The affine comparison uses a bounded affine actor. Validation, not final testing, selects the checkpoint. Architecture, action radius, training sampler and final economic problem are shared where the comparison calls for them. Differences in optimizer work are exposed by time, visits and update counts rather than disguised as equal iterations.

Every primary final row uses 8,192 independent paths and 1,024 time cells. A family of 4,000 one-sided statements is allocated before final testing and covers both signs of every reported stochastic endpoint. Tables label the raw average as the mean paired statistic. The lower and upper endpoints include the empirical Bernstein margin, continuous-time transfer, tail clipping and arithmetic accounts. Across-seed ranges describe the ten recorded fitted objects; they are not confidence limits for every possible future training run.

Training cost is only one component of work. The table reports verification separately because proving that a small difference is positive can be more expensive than constructing the candidate. Deployment timing concerns the actor proposal on a batch of 256 states; it excludes physical sensing, drift-integral observation and private-randomization infrastructure. It therefore must not be read as total implementation cost. The full record retains peak process memory, simulator-state visits, critic and actor updates, selected iteration, all checkpoint times and final evaluation costs.

The supplementary ablations remove the critic altogether by using raw rollout costates, remove either value-level or costate regression, change the number of block updates, and change network width. The radius frontier trains new actors at 0.05 and 0.15; it does not merely rescale a correction trained at 0.10. Checkpoint frontiers use the same full final path count as the primary study. Their selected checkpoints are determined by the work clock, not by the final payoff. These experiments test the mechanism in Proposition~\ref{prop:r12costate} and expose cases where its potential variance reduction does not repay its approximation or optimization cost.

The adoption account applies \eqref{eq:r12fee} at fees of zero, 0.05, 0.10 and 0.20 percent of gross withdrawals. \subsection{Absolute policy accuracy in an independent nonlinear instance}
The one-state member of the same model family admits a strong independent numerical reference without an exact homothetic or quadratic solution. We solve its monotone implicit HJB system on three nested grids and compare the largest grid with a wider state domain. The solver uses full-box feasible maximization and banded linear solves; no neural critic enters its target values. Fixed-policy solves evaluate the learned Markov feedbacks on the same grid.

The reference gives an absolute policy-loss diagnostic in a genuinely nonlinear instance and separates value accuracy from beating a feasible schedule. Its boundary approximation and finite-grid errors are exposed through domain and mesh sensitivity, not promoted to a rigorous continuous-domain certificate. The scalar grid feedback is also kept distinct from the innovation-history controller in the high-dimensional theorem. The latter's global optimum comparison remains the explicit $G_d-L_a$ account. An independent scalar reference does not itself prove that a 50-dimensional neural policy is near optimal.

\subsection{Scope of the computational claim}
The evidence identifies several separate objects: the NBO evaluation--improvement map, an action-loss bound conditional on its evaluation accuracy, policy-specific economic verification, direct method comparisons, and the cost of a specified implementation decision. None of them makes universal superiority a premise. The capital transfer and observation propositions are model-specific. Recursive utility, endogenous preference adjustment, stopping, temporal selves and strategic deviations retain the models and verification accounts developed elsewhere in this paper and its supplement; their hypotheses are not silently supplied by the capital proof.
